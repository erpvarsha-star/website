## The short answer

All three are medium-carbon alloy steels that respond well to quenching and tempering. They suit forged steering and suspension parts that carry load and shock.

In simple terms, EN19 and 42CrMo4 are close relatives. Both rely on chromium and molybdenum. EN24 adds nickel, which gives deeper hardening and more toughness in thicker sections. The right grade depends on the load, the section size and what your drawing or customer standard already names.

## Why steering parts need an alloy steel

A steering part sees repeated loads, sudden shocks and torque. A tie rod end, steering lever, idler arm or drop arm must resist fatigue and must not snap if it is overloaded.

Plain carbon steel can do some of this work in small, lightly loaded parts. Alloying elements let the steel harden more evenly through its section, so that the core as well as the surface reaches the strength you need after heat treatment. That depth of hardening is called hardenability, and it is the main reason to move from a carbon steel to an alloy grade.

## What each grade brings

**EN19.** A chromium-molybdenum steel. Chromium helps hardenability and wear resistance. Molybdenum helps the steel keep its strength and resist a brittle condition during tempering. EN19 is a well-known general choice for shafts, levers and other stressed parts of moderate section.

**42CrMo4.** The European designation for a steel of very similar character, listed in the EN standards by that name. It is widely specified on drawings from European customers. In practice many buyers treat it as the same family as EN19, and as a close relative of AISI 4140.

**EN24.** A nickel-chromium-molybdenum steel. The added nickel improves hardenability further and helps toughness, especially in heavier sections and where shock loads are high. It is often chosen when a part is large, or when a bigger safety margin is wanted.

Treat these as families, not exact twins. Chemistry limits differ between standards, so never assume two grades are interchangeable without checking the standard named on your drawing.

## How to choose a grade for a forged steering part

Work through four questions.

**1. What does the drawing or your customer standard already say?** If a grade is named, that is usually the end of the choice. Changing grade needs your engineering approval and often a new PPAP submission.

**2. How thick is the section?** In thin sections, EN19 or 42CrMo4 reaches the target properties through the whole part. In heavy sections the centre may not harden as well, and EN24 becomes more attractive.

**3. How severe is the duty?** Parts exposed to shock, such as steering levers on heavy vehicles, justify a tougher steel. Parts under steady moderate load may not.

**4. What does the extra cost buy?** A more highly alloyed steel costs more per kilogram. Pay for it only where the section or the duty needs it.

## Heat treatment matters as much as the grade

The same grade can give very different results depending on how it is heat treated. Specify the process and the result you need, not only the steel.

Typical routes for these grades include normalizing after forging to refine the grain, then quenching and tempering to reach the final strength and toughness. Stress relieving may follow machining. Induction hardening can add a hard, wear-resistant surface on a bearing area.

Ask your supplier to state hardness range, the test method and the sampling plan on the control plan. Also ask for grain size results, because a fine, even grain supports toughness.

## What to specify on the drawing

- **The grade and the standard it comes from.** Write both.
- **The heat treatment condition.** For example, quenched and tempered, with the hardness range you need.
- **The testing you require.** Hardness, grain size, and crack testing on safety-critical parts.
- **Material certificate type.** Say whether you need a 3.1 or 3.2 certificate.
- **Traceability.** Ask that each part trace to the steel heat.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is EN19 the same as 42CrMo4?**
They are closely related chromium-molybdenum steels, and many buyers treat them as the same family. Chemistry limits differ between standards, so check the standard on your drawing before substituting.

**Is EN24 stronger than EN19?**
EN24 hardens more deeply and is tougher in heavy sections. Final strength depends on the heat treatment as much as the grade.

**Can I swap one grade for another to save cost?**
Not without engineering approval. A grade change can alter strength, toughness and fatigue behaviour, and usually needs a new approval submission.

**Do all three grades suit tie rod ends?**
Each can. The choice depends on section size, duty and your drawing. Varsha Forgings forges tie rod ends on their own, and also makes tie rods as complete assemblies.
