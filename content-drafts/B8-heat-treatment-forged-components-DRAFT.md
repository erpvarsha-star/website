## The short answer

A forging gets its shape from the press or hammer. It gets its working strength from heat treatment. The same steel can be soft or hard, brittle or tough, depending on how it is heated and cooled.

So heat treatment belongs on your drawing and in your RFQ. If you leave it open, you leave the result open. This guide explains the main processes in plain terms and lists what to specify.

## Why forged parts are heat treated

Hot forging leaves the steel with a coarse or uneven structure in places. Parts cool at different rates, so the structure varies from one area to another. Heat treatment resets and refines that structure.

The goal depends on the part. Some parts need balanced strength and toughness all the way through. Others need a hard surface to resist wear, and a tougher core to resist shock. Others only need stress removed so they stay stable during machining.

## The main processes, in plain terms

**Normalizing.** The part is heated, then cooled in still air. This refines the grain and makes the structure more uniform. It is often used to prepare a forging for machining or for later hardening.

**Quench and temper.** The part is heated, then cooled fast in a quenching medium to harden it. It is then reheated at a lower level, called tempering, to reduce brittleness and set the final balance of strength and toughness. A double quench and temper repeats the cycle to refine the result further, where the part and grade call for it.

**Carburizing.** The surface of a low-carbon steel absorbs carbon at high heat, then the part is hardened. The result is a hard, wear-resistant case over a tougher core. Vacuum carburizing does this in a vacuum furnace, which gives close control of the process. Gears and shafts are typical users.

**Induction hardening.** An electric coil heats only the surface or a chosen zone, and the part is quenched straight away. It hardens a local area, such as a journal or a ball-stud seat, and leaves the rest as it was.

**Stress relieving.** The part is heated to a moderate level and held, then cooled slowly. It does not change hardness much. It removes locked-in stress from forging, hardening or heavy machining, so the part keeps its shape.

**Annealing.** The part is heated and cooled slowly to make it softer and easier to machine. It is used when a part needs to be soft for cutting.

## Which process for which part

Use this as a starting guide. Your design team sets the final choice.

| Need | Often suited to |
|---|---|
| Even strength and toughness through the section | Quench and temper |
| Refined, uniform structure before machining | Normalizing |
| Hard wear surface, tough core, such as gears | Carburizing |
| Hard local zone, such as a journal | Induction hardening |
| Stable shape after heavy machining | Stress relieving |
| Soft, easy-to-cut condition | Annealing |

## What buyers should specify

Put these items on the drawing or in the RFQ.

1. **Material grade.** Heat treatment response depends on the grade. Name it.
2. **Process and condition.** For example, quench and temper to a stated hardness range, or normalized.
3. **Hardness requirement.** State the range and the test method, such as Brinell or Rockwell, and where on the part to test.
4. **Case requirement, if any.** For carburized or induction-hardened parts, state the hardened depth and the surface hardness.
5. **Test locations and frequency.** Say how many parts per batch get checked, and where.
6. **Microstructure and grain size.** If it matters, state the method, such as ASTM E112 for grain size.
7. **Distortion and straightening limits.** Hardening can move a part. Agree on how much movement is acceptable, and where machining allowance sits.
8. **Records.** Ask for heat treatment records linked to the batch and the steel heat.

## How to check what you receive

Ask your supplier how each requirement is verified.

- **Hardness testing** on sample parts, using Brinell, Rockwell or micro-Vickers methods.
- **Grain size checks** under a microscope.
- **Chemical analysis** of the steel to confirm the grade.
- **Crack testing** after heat treatment, because quenching can cause cracks in rare cases.
- **Traceability.** Each part should link to its steel heat and its heat treatment batch.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between normalizing and quench and temper?**
Normalizing cools the part in air and refines the grain. Quench and temper cools it fast, then reheats it, to reach a chosen strength and toughness.

**What is the difference between carburizing and induction hardening?**
Carburizing adds carbon to the surface before hardening. Induction hardening heats a chosen surface zone with a coil and quenches it.

**Why is stress relieving needed?**
It removes locked-in stress so the part holds its shape during and after machining.
