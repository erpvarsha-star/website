## Why the certificate is the first filter

European automotive buyers usually ask for IATF 16949 before they discuss price. It is the automotive quality management standard, and it builds on ISO 9001 with extra requirements for defect prevention, supply chain control and continual improvement.

For a buyer sourcing forged components from India, the certificate answers one question quickly: does this supplier run a recognised automotive quality system? It does not answer every question, so use it as the first filter and not the last.

## What the certificate tells you

A valid IATF 16949 certificate tells you that an outside certification body audited the supplier's system against the standard. In practice it points to:

- Documented control of processes, from incoming steel to dispatch
- Planning methods such as APQP and control plans
- A route for customer approval of parts through PPAP
- Corrective action and internal audit routines
- Supplier management, including the control of outside partners

## What the certificate does not tell you

A certificate covers a site and a scope. It does not prove that your particular part is made well. Three gaps matter most to buyers.

**Scope.** Check that the certificate covers the plant that will make your part, and the processes you need.

**Part capability.** A system audit is not a part approval. PPAP evidence, capability data and sample parts do that job.

**Day-to-day practice.** A certificate is a snapshot. Visits, audits and production data show whether the system is alive.

## Questions to ask beyond the certificate

Use these as an audit starter for an Indian forging supplier:

1. Which plant and scope does the certificate cover?
2. Which PPAP levels do you submit, and can you show an example package?
3. How do you run statistical process control, and what capability do you hold on key characteristics?
4. How do you trace each part to its steel heat?
5. Which processes are in-house, and which go to partners?
6. Which material certificate do you issue?
7. How do you handle engineering changes and deviations?
8. Can we visit the plant, or take a virtual tour first?

## Supporting certificates and systems

IATF 16949 usually sits beside other management standards. European buyers also look for ISO 9001 for quality, ISO 14001 for environmental management and ISO 45001 for occupational health and safety. Material data reporting through IMDS is common in automotive supply, and many buyers ask for it at the quotation stage.

## Material certificates for European buyers

European buyers commonly expect material certificates to EN 10204. Type 3.1 is issued by the manufacturer's authorised representative, and type 3.2 adds validation by an independent party or the customer's representative. Confirm which type your drawing requires before you order.

## Working with a supplier in a different time zone

Distance is a communication question more than a quality question. Agree who your contact is, how drawings and revisions are shared, and how quickly each side answers. A written RFQ sequence helps both sides. Agree a feasibility response time before you send the first drawing.

## Planning the first order with a new supplier

Treat the first order as a trial of the whole relationship, not only of the part. Start with a single drawing, agree the PPAP level, and confirm the sample plan before tooling begins. Ask for a written timeline that lists each stage: feasibility, quotation, die making, samples, approval and series supply.

Review the first samples against the control plan, and note any open questions in writing. Once the part is approved, agree how engineering changes will be requested and confirmed, so both sides work from the same revision.
