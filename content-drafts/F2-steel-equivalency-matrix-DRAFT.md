## Page copy

**Read this first.** Cross-standard equivalents are indicative only. No row in this table is an exact match: carbon, manganese, chromium, nickel and molybdenum ranges, residual-element limits and required mechanical properties differ from one standard to the next, and a grade that is close in one respect can be outside the other standard's limits in another. Before you rely on a grade for a design or an order, confirm it against the actual standard and the specification that applies to your part.

### H2: How to Read This Matrix

Engineers buying forgings across borders meet the same steel under several names. A drawing from a European plant may say 42CrMo4. A North American drawing may say AISI 4140. An older British drawing may say EN19. This matrix lines up the carbon and alloy steels that Varsha Forgings lists, so you can see the family a grade belongs to and check that your drawing and our quotation mean the same material.

Read it as a map, not a contract. Equivalent grades are close in chemistry and properties, not identical. Limits on carbon, manganese, chromium, molybdenum, nickel, sulphur and phosphorus differ between standards, and so do the tests each standard requires. The grade on your drawing always governs. This page is general engineering reference and not a statement that any two grades can be substituted on your part.

### H2: Which Grade Family Does What

General engineering background, not a Varsha claim.

**Medium-carbon steels (EN8, EN9, AISI 1045).** Plain carbon grades that respond to hardening and tempering. They suit parts where moderate strength is enough.

**Chromium-molybdenum and nickel-chromium-molybdenum steels (EN19, EN24, 42CrMo4, AISI 4140).** Through-hardening alloy steels used for shafts, levers and highly loaded parts, where strength and fatigue resistance after quench and temper matter. EN24 and its 34CrNiMo6 family add nickel for toughness at higher section sizes.

**Case-hardening steels (20MnCr5, 16MnCr5, 20MoCr4E, SAE8620).** Low-carbon alloy steels designed to be carburized, which gives a hard wear-resistant surface over a tough core. Gears and shafts are typical uses.

**High-carbon chromium steel (EN31).** A high-carbon chromium grade of the bearing-steel family, hard and wear resistant.

**Manganese steels (SAE1541).** Medium-carbon steels with a higher manganese content, giving more strength and hardenability than plain carbon steel of similar carbon.

**Pressure and low-temperature forging grades (ASTM A105, LF2, F11, F22).** Specified under pressure-equipment and piping standards, for example the standards commonly cover flanges, fittings and valve bodies in pressure piping. This describes how the standards are generally used, not a statement of Varsha Forgings' markets.

### H2: Specifying a Grade Without Ambiguity

When you send a drawing, give the standard as well as the grade, for example EN 10083-3 or SAE J404. Name the supply condition: as-forged, normalized, or quenched and tempered. State the hardness window or mechanical properties you need. Say whether you require an EN 10204 type 3.1 certificate, or 3.2 with third-party witness.

For case-hardening grades, state the case depth, surface hardness and core hardness, and the heat-treatment process. Varsha Forgings offers vacuum carburizing, induction hardening, double quench and temper, normalizing and stress relieving, and tests hardness by Brinell, Rockwell and micro-Vickers, and grain size to ASTM E112.

### H2: Frequently Asked Questions

**Is EN19 the same as 4140?**
They are close and often treated as equivalent for engineering purposes, but limits differ between the standards. Confirm against your drawing.

**Does Varsha Forgings forge stainless steel?**
No. We forge carbon and alloy steels only.

**Can I replace a grade with its equivalent without approval?**
Not without your design authority agreeing. This page is a reference, not a substitution approval.

**What certificate comes with the material?**
EN 10204 type 3.1, and 3.2 on request.
