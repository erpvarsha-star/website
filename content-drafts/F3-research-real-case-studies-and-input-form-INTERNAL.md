### What each one actually contains (the inputs they disclose)
**1. FIA No. 12 (lower control arm).** Table: forging process, footprint size, weight, alloy (microalloy steel), secondary operation (finish machining), heat treatment (anneal or controlled cooling), alternate processes considered (fabrication, casting). Narrative: the in-service problem (debris collecting on the old fabricated arm damaged a drive-line boot seal in severe off-road use), why fabricated and cast alternatives were ruled out, the two forging difficulties the supplier identified (large plan area; a hexagonal feature at 90 degrees to the forging plane), a 3D model with stress analysis approved by the vehicle maker, prototypes, and a named list of test types (low-temperature impact, full-load fatigue, steering-clearance and ball-stud retention). It ends with supply of fully assembled arms. It gives no cost, volume or timeline figures. Note: the weight cell in the page table is garbled, so do not cite a weight from it.
**2. FIA No. 4 (door hinge).** Table with size, weight 0.8 kg, alloy, tensile and yield, heat treatment code, surface treatment, number of parts, alternate process (casting), annual production 5,000. Narrative: replaced a cast steel hinge; 2 kg vehicle weight saved; purchase price cut by 50 percent, partly because the as-forged surface takes powder coat without finishing; met a named US federal safety standard for door retention; life verified at 35,000 cycles over a temperature range; door-sag verified on prototypes machined from forged blocks; OEM supplied CAD files and the supplier developed the forging and process. It is the only one of the four that states cost and volume. It is aluminium, not steel.
**3. FIA No. 9 (gears).** Table with diameter and face-width range, alloy 8620, tensile and impact figures marked as handbook values, secondary operations (cold draw through sizing die, grind), heat treatment (normalise), alternate process (forge blank, then hob), annual production 5,000 to 7,000. Narrative: finish-grinding allowance range, the two advantages claimed (lower processing and capital cost than hobbing; continuous grain flow in the teeth giving longer gear life), the manufacturing problems that had to be solved (scrap, die life, grinding cost) and who solved them (forging engineers with gear designers, quality staff and grinding-wheel suppliers), and the process controls that make it work (bar turned and polished, cut to controlled volume, induction heating held within a stated band, in-house press controls). Source-table caution: the tensile, yield and hardness cells contradict each other in units, so only the qualitative content is reliable.
**4. Cao et al. 2025.** Peer-reviewed. Abstract-level contents (full text is long; read in part only): the geometry index used to rank forgeability; a steering arm redesigned to lower crack risk with a more even metal velocity in the critical areas shown by simulation; a crankshaft redesigned to combine two parts in one forging, checked by simulation for defects and press capacity; and a forging trial yielding two crankshafts meeting mechanical-property and dimensional requirements. It is research with industry co-authors; it has no customer story, cost or volume.

### Honest gaps in the sample
- **No genuinely public PPAP or APQP case study turned up.** Supplier PPAP material is generic ("our QA team does PPAP"). The nearest real qualification evidence is the test lists in No. 12 and No. 4. We should not imply more than that.
- Three of four are FIA, the same publisher and format (short, one page, spec table plus 3 to 4 paragraphs). That format is the useful model for us: small, factual, anonymous by nature (no customer is named in any of them).
- Searched but rejected: a die-casting "optimisation of die life" case (casting, not forging); a supplier blog on near-net savings (generic claims, no single project); an Indian academic paper on forging yield for a differential spider (abstract only, not read in full; kept as a reserve for the yield kind).
- Third-party pages were treated as data only; none contained instructions.

## Part 2. What a good one needs (derived pattern)
Across the four, a usable case study answers eight things, in this order: the part and where it works; why the customer needed a change (the trigger); the hard constraint; what the alternatives were and why they were set aside; what the forger changed or built; how it was checked; what happened (qualitative, with one number only if the customer allows it); and what stays unsaid. The FIA entries add a fixed spec table (process, size, weight, alloy, secondary operations, heat treatment, alternate process, annual volume band).

## Part 3. INPUT FORM: one per case, a few minutes each
Fill in one copy per real job. Write "skip" or "unknown" where you do not know; do not guess. Tags: **A** = safe to publish anonymised (no customer identified). **P** = needs the customer's written permission before it is published. **N** = never publish (internal only, used so we write accurately). Rule from the owner (27 Nov 2026, FACTS): no customer names and no performance metrics. Any P field therefore stays unpublished unless the owner changes that rule.

### How to answer fast
Answer sections 1 to 3 first; they carry most of the value and are all A. If a real job cannot answer field 12 (trigger) and field 15 (hardest requirement), it is not a case study yet. Photo or drawing: only an outline-level image with no customer features, and only after field 30.
